<div class="page-kicker">01 / Research program</div>

I lead a computational communication research program on information access, credibility, and inequality in health and politics. My work explains how people's choices, platform selection, and social connections shape public information.

These four questions organize studies of political search, health information, credibility, and AI answers. Throughout, I distinguish information availability, opportunities for exposure, and audience responses.

<section class="content-module" markdown="1">
<div class="module-kicker">Research pillar 01</div>

## People

Credibility judgments are also acts of political communication. In my [Mass Communication and Society study](https://doi.org/10.1080/15205436.2020.1789661), I distinguished definitions of “fake news” that describe false content from accusations aimed at political and media opponents. The accusations were associated with affective polarization. This distinction brings partisan identity and attempts to discredit opponents into the study of credibility, alongside factual accuracy. I led the conceptual framing, survey design, and analysis of open responses.

Related collaborative work in [PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0328550) develops a typology of how people object to news commentary. My manuscript with Tanner Smith, under review at *Political Communication*, examines framing and nationalization in pink slime outlets presented as local news. Both projects examine how people contest the legitimacy of public information.

</section>

<section class="content-module" markdown="1">
<div class="module-kicker">Research pillar 02</div>

## Platform and social curation

Apparent search bias can reflect the questions people ask as well as platform selection. In my [WebSci 2025 study](https://doi.org/10.1145/3717867.3717888), query slant was the primary source of differences in Google results across three political topics, while authoritative sources appeared across searches. As sole author, I separated query choices from variation associated with users' political identities. This separation changes how we interpret apparent search bias. A related [UMAP Adjunct paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/3708319.3733704) examines search results during the 2020 Black Lives Matter protests.

<div id="health-information-networks" markdown="1">
Health information research needs to connect what is produced with the networks through which people could encounter it. Drawing on the public communication environment framework, my [Journal of Medical Internet Research study](https://doi.org/10.2196/63864) links colorectal cancer racial equity information with expertise and brokerage. I led the conceptual framing, study design, and operational definitions of equity information disseminators and broker accounts. Broker accounts were defined by a majority of followers lacking other expert connections within the studied network. These ties indicate opportunities for exposure, not evidence of reading or action.

The content analysis revealed a second constraint on access: outcome disparities and calls to action were much more common than practical screening information or symptoms in the posts we coded.
</div>

<section class="content-module" markdown="1">
<div class="module-kicker">Research pillar 03</div>

## Information

What researchers can find shapes what they can conclude about an information environment. For my [JMIR Medical Informatics study](https://doi.org/10.2196/37862), I led the research design and developed a retrieval approach combining word embeddings, recommendation networks, and human relevance judgments. It recovered YouTube health videos expressed in colloquial terms or preparation brand names that clinical seed terms could miss. The method improved recall while limiting precision loss; it evaluates research retrieval, not patient search behavior.

{% include research/retrieval-visual.html %}

In my [International Journal of Qualitative Methods article](https://doi.org/10.1177/16094069261486252), my collaborators and I introduced the Alignment and Usefulness Rating Audit, or AURA. It separates three evaluation questions: Do topics generated by a model correspond with themes coded by people? Are they useful to domain experts? Do they reveal additional themes? Our demonstration found broad correspondence but limited evidence of additional thematic discovery. These are distinct contributions a computational method might make to qualitative analysis.

I also supervise a longitudinal audit of Google AI Overviews, led by students, across English and Spanish queries. It examines answer availability and the stability of responses and sources; the full paper is under review at ICWSM 2027. A separate study examines uncertainty, guidance, and safeguards in AI health answers. Both establish what systems provide before we draw conclusions about effects on readers.

</section>

<section class="content-module" markdown="1">
<div class="module-kicker">Research pillar 04</div>

## Impact

Information availability and audience response require different evidence. In a collaborative [American Journal of Preventive Medicine study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2025.107998), I helped analyze adults' written reactions to cannabis health warnings. Guided by the Extended Parallel Process Model, we combined topic modeling, human coding, and statistical analysis. Maladaptive reactions were associated with greater reactance, less accurate beliefs about cannabis harms, and lower intentions to prevent harm. These associations do not establish that the reactions caused the beliefs or intentions.

My credibility research likewise links judgments with affective polarization without treating that association as causal. Current work extends the audience question to how people interpret uncertain AI health answers and possible next steps.

</section>
